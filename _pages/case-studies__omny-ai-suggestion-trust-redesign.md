*Case study · Omny AI*

# Making AI suggestions useful without making them automatic

Omny AI generates product benefits and use cases for brands and agencies selling on Amazon, but unreviewed suggestions were entering the work with too much implied authority. Tcules redesigned the interaction so AI proposals remained separate from committed content until a person reviewed and accepted them, with clearer ownership of what entered the listing.

Users were losing trust when suggestions they had not properly reviewed turned out to be wrong. Tcules changed how those suggestions entered the work.

## At a glance {#case-context}

| Product | Omny AI, Amazon seller software that uses AI to write product benefits and use cases, for brands and agencies. |
|---|---|
| Our role | AI interaction design, information architecture and control patterns for AI-written listing content. |
| The key question | How can AI suggestions reach a listing only after a person has reviewed and accepted them? |
| What we delivered | An interaction where AI suggestions stay visibly separate until the user chooses what goes into the listing, with purple for AI content and blue for the person's own. |

Related Tcules services: [Trust, Control and Recovery](/services/ai-product-ux/trust-control-recovery) and [AI Product UX](/services/ai-product-ux).

## Who was accountable when AI suggestions entered the listing? {#the-interface-had-quietly-changed-who-was-accoun}
The original design filled the content area with the AI’s suggestions before the person did anything. Accepting them required no deliberate choice. People could move ahead without giving the generated content the review it needed.

The Omny team's own description of it was exact: users were holding Omny accountable for suggestions they had not properly reviewed.

The default had made acceptance automatic. Tcules gave choosing a suggestion its own action, so users had a clear point at which to take responsibility for the content.

## Blank by default, and a click to accept {#blank-by-default-and-a-click-to-accept}

Tcules rebuilt the interaction as a collaboration rather than a handover. The final content area starts empty. The suggestions sit in their own panel beside it. Adding one takes a deliberate click.

The deliberate click gives acceptance a clear place in the workflow. The person chooses what belongs in the listing, with the suggested material still available beside it.

The wider lesson is unglamorous and worth stating plainly. Where an AI feature makes someone accountable for its output, the effort of accepting a suggestion should not be lower than the effort of reading it.

## Purple is the machine, blue is the person {#purple-is-the-machine-blue-is-the-person}

The second decision runs through the whole product. Tcules gave authorship a colour: purple for anything the AI generated, suggested or did automatically, blue for anything a person typed, chose or overrode.

The same distinction carried across budget allocation, the budget strategy timeline and product content management. Purple identified material extracted by the system; blue identified material a person added. Users could see authorship while reviewing the content.

Before any of the interface work, Tcules examined the raw data patterns to find the relationships that actually mattered, so the structure came from the data rather than from the shape of the existing screens. Each element was then designed against the question a user would be asking at that point: how to decide a budget allocation, which indicator belongs on this screen, what this number is being compared against.

## Choosing the suggestion changed the relationship {#what-the-work-establishes-and-what-it-does-not}

Separating suggestions from committed content gave users a clearer role in the result. Omny reported that users became more aware of how they were using the AI and more forgiving when a suggestion was wrong.

The same authorship language carried across content and budget tools, making the distinction between machine suggestions and human decisions visible throughout the experience.

## The trade-off this case is really about {#the-trade-off-this-case-is-really-about}

The added action needed to earn its place. Tcules kept suggestions close to the work and made acceptance deliberate, giving users a useful check without rebuilding the task around an approval process.

The useful question is not how to remove steps. It is which step is carrying the accountability, and whether the person taking it can tell that they are.

The distinction between removing steps and preserving accountability is central to Tcules' work on [trust, control and recovery](/services/ai-product-ux/trust-control-recovery) and, more broadly, [AI product UX](/services/ai-product-ux). The commerce side of the domain is covered under [commerce and transactional products](/expertise/commerce-transactional-products).

## What deliberate acceptance changed {#delivery-impact}

| Impact dimension | What changed |
|---|---|
| Human accountability | An AI suggestion required a deliberate acceptance action before it became committed listing content. |
| Provenance | A consistent visual distinction separated machine-generated material from content a person typed, selected or overrode. |
| What Omny reported | Users became more aware of how they were using the AI and more forgiving when a suggestion was wrong. |
