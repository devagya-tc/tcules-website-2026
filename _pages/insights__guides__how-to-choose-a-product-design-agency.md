# How to choose a product design agency and test the fit

By [Rakesh Chitroda](/insights/authors/rakesh-chitroda) · Updated 26 October 2026 · 9 min read

To choose a product design agency, match the kind of partner to the gap in your team, run six checks before you hire, and test the fit on one small project. This guide walks through each step, with the questions to ask on the first call.

A portfolio shows the finished screens. It rarely shows who questioned the brief, which options were dropped, or whether the design survived contact with engineering. The right partner widens what your product can become, as well as how it looks.

**Key takeaways**

- Start with the gap in your team. An in-house hire, a freelancer, a development company, AI tools and a product design studio each fill a different one.
- Run six checks before you hire, from whether a partner questions your assumptions to whether their case studies hold up to a closer look.
- On the first call, ask who you would work with each day and which part of your brief they would question first.
- Test the fit with a small pilot project on one problem, judged against the same six checks.

## Product design agency, freelancer or in-house hire: which fits your gap? {#partner-type}

Product design is bought in several ways. Each fills a different gap in your team, so start with the gap.

| If you are missing… | Consider | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Someone to own the product for years | Hiring in-house | Years of embedded product knowledge matter most |
| One well-defined task with a clear direction | A freelance specialist | One expert, light coordination |
| Engineering capacity against a finished specification | A development company | Build capacity and scale matter most |
| Quick drafts of screens or a clickable idea to react to | AI design and prototyping tools | Cheap to try, though someone still has to judge the output against real users and real data |
| Product thinking across research, design, systems and code | A product design studio | One team carries the product from first idea to working software, so less is lost between research, design and code |

These options combine well. Many teams keep an in-house designer and bring in a studio for research, a design system or a new product. Others use AI tools to explore first, then hand over to a designer.

## How to evaluate a design agency: six checks before you hire {#six-checks}

### 1. Do they question what your product assumes?

Good product design starts below the screens: who the users are, what they are trying to do and what the software takes for granted. A partner who designs straight from your brief designs your assumptions too.

**How to check:** Ask for an example where research changed the brief. After the first call, ask what they would challenge in yours. A partner with nothing to say may have understood very little, or may want to please you.

### 2. Are they at home in demanding software?

Business software is hard because its rules, roles and exceptions all have to fit together. A partner used to short, single-purpose apps may simplify by removing what your users rely on.

**How to check:** Ask them to walk through a past workflow with several roles in it. Listen for who can do what, how records change and what happens when something goes wrong.

### 3. Do they show you the options they dropped?

A single polished direction hides the thinking behind it. A partner worth hiring shows what else they considered, why they chose one option and what each would have cost your users or your team.

**How to check:** Ask to see one choice from a past project with the alternatives side by side. If every project shows a single answer, the choosing happened somewhere you cannot inspect.

### 4. Can they work inside your team?

Design goes better when the people doing it work directly with your product managers, designers and engineers, in your tools.

**How to check:** Ask who your day-to-day contact will be, whether that person does the work, and how the team changes when the work changes. Ask about minimum terms and how much notice you need to scale up or down.

### 5. Does their design reach the code?

A design that lives only in a design file can lose its purpose on the way to production. Partners who prototype in code, or whose engineers build from their designs, catch those problems while they are still cheap to fix.

**How to check:** Ask whether their designers prototype in code and how they hand work to engineers. Ask whether they have taken a design of their own through to working software.

### 6. Does their work hold up to a closer look?

Studios write their own case studies. Look for details that are hard to invent: what the client already had, what the studio did, what the client's own team did and what changed.

**How to check:** Read two or three case studies end to end, read independent reviews, and ask to speak to a past client whose product resembles yours. Start with Tcules' own [case studies](/case-studies) and apply the same test to them.

## Design agency red flags to look for in the pitch {#red-flags}

Most warning signs show up before any contract, if you know where to look. Each one below is the failed version of a check above.

- Every case study shows one polished direction, with no options set aside and no reason given for the choice.
- Nobody can give an example of research changing a client's brief.
- The people on the pitch call are not the people who would do the work, and nobody will name your day-to-day contact.
- The design stops at a file, with no clear route into your engineers' code or their own.
- Case studies skip what the client already had and what the client's own team did.
- Minimum terms, notice periods and what happens when the work changes stay vague after you have asked.

One flag is a question to raise on the call. Several together usually mean the fit is wrong.

## Questions to ask a design agency on the first call {#first-call}

- Who would we work with each day, and what would they do themselves?
- What would you want to know about our users before designing anything?
- Which part of our brief would you question first?
- How do you show us options and the reasons behind them?
- How does your design reach our engineers, or your own?
- How is the work priced, and what happens when the work changes?
- What would make you tell us we should hire someone else?

## Test a design partner with a small project before you commit {#test-the-fit}

Real work shows you what a presentation hides. Before you commit, give the partner one problem that stands on its own. A single workflow, one feature or an audit of one area all work. Agree up front what the project must answer and how you will judge it.

Then hold the result, and the way the team worked, against the six checks above. A good small project leaves you with something useful whether or not you go further. [How a pilot project works with Tcules](/how-we-work) sets out what we agree up front and how we work.

## The checks applied to a real project: Dealpath's listing workflow {#checks-on-a-real-project}

Checks are easier to run once you have seen them work on a real project. Here is one of ours, read against three of them. Hold any studio's case studies, ours included, to the same standard.

Dealpath makes deal-management software for real-estate acquisitions teams. Its Add to pipeline action opened Create deal straight away, which assumed every listing was a new deal.

We reversed the order, so people search for and attach an existing deal first, with a new deal kept as a deliberate fallback. That is check 1: questioning what the product assumed.

Check 3 shows up in the listing card. Removing fields would have made a cleaner card, and we set that option aside because acquisitions teams need the property data to judge an opportunity. We reordered the card into three zones instead: image, source and status; property identity and highlights; core values for comparison.

Check 6 asks who did what. Tcules audited the product and redesigned the workflow, and Dealpath's own team owned implementation. Of the four actions on a listing, only the pipeline action was redesigned; the others were largely kept as they were. The [Dealpath case study](/case-studies/enterprise-workflow-redesign-dealpath-real-estate) sets out the rest, so you can test it yourself.

## How to choose a product design agency: questions buyers ask {#faq}

### How do I choose a product design agency?

Start with the gap in your team, because a studio, a freelancer, an in-house hire and a development company each fill a different one. Then run the six checks in this guide and ask the first-call questions. Finish with a small project that shows how the team works before you commit to more.

### What should I ask a design agency in the first call?

Ask who you would work with each day and what they would do themselves. Then ask which part of your brief they would question first, and how their design reaches your engineers. A partner with nothing to challenge in your brief may have understood very little of it.

### Should I hire a design agency or a freelancer?

Hire a freelancer for one well-defined task with a clear direction, where one expert and light coordination are enough. Choose a product design agency or studio when the work spans research, design, systems and code, or needs several people at once. Many teams combine the two.

### How can I test a design partner before committing?

Give them one problem that stands on its own, such as a single workflow, one feature or an audit of one area. Agree up front what the project must answer and how you will judge it. Then hold the result, and the way the team worked, against the six checks.

### How do I choose a UX design agency for a SaaS product?

Run the same six checks, and give extra weight to two of them: experience with demanding multi-role software, and a clear route from design into code. Then compare named studios side by side.
