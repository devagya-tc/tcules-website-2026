# Landing page design best practices, from the ad's promise to confirmation

By [Yash Bhatt](/insights/authors/yash-bhatt) · Published 2 May 2025 · Updated 26 October 2026 · 11 min read

Landing page design best practices come down to one idea: keep the promise that brought the visitor, from the click through to the confirmation. This guide covers what to decide before design, how to order the page, how to use proof and what to measure, with an example from our own site.

A landing page works when the right visitor recognises their situation, understands the change on offer and can test the claims. Then they take a next step that fits their stage.

That path starts in the search result, advertisement, email or referral that created the visit. It ends when the visitor sees their action confirmed and knows what happens next.

A landing page is a focused route from one source and one expectation to one next action. The visitor reads the headline, layout, testimonial and form as a single argument for continuing.

So the promise has to hold before the layout can help. When it holds, the page becomes the first thing a visitor learns about how your product behaves.

**Key takeaways**

- A landing page works when the right visitor recognises their situation, understands the change on offer and can test the claims.
- Decide the offer, the visitor and the one primary outcome before design begins.
- Write the story before arranging the interface, and put the proof next to the claim it supports.
- One action can appear more than once, provided every prompt serves the same decision.
- Measure qualified submissions, sales acceptance and recurring objections alongside the conversion rate.

## B2B landing page design: three answers before any layout {#set-the-first-principles-before-the-page-section}

Three questions need plain-language answers before design starts.

### What kind of product or offer is this? {#what-kind-of-product-or-offer-is-this}

An unfamiliar category must first become understandable. A differentiated product has to make the difference concrete. A service in a familiar market has to show why its way of taking responsibility matters. Positioning decides what the page must establish before it asks for trust.

### Which visitor is the page for? {#which-visitor-is-the-page-for}

Demographics alone rarely explain a high-consideration purchase. Capture the visitor's product situation, trigger and responsibility. Capture their doubts and internal constraints, and the proof they can show colleagues. A founder weighing a 0-to-1 partner and a product leader weighing modernisation may value the same capability for different reasons.

### What is the primary outcome? {#what-is-the-primary-outcome}

Awareness, qualification, enquiry, trial and purchase are different outcomes. A page can offer secondary actions, but its main argument should lead to one next step. Agree that step with your team and leadership before debating the button label.

## SaaS landing page design: what belongs in the first screen {#start-with-the-acquisition-sentence}

Write down the promise that caused the visit. Then write the question the visitor is trying to answer. Both should meet in the first screen. If they do not, the visitor has to stop and re-orient before reading on.

For a high-consideration software service, “what do you do?” is only the opening question. The buyer may also be asking:

- Do you understand a product like ours?
- Which part of the problem can you own?
- Can you work with our product and engineering teams?
- Is your proof close enough to our situation?
- What happens if we start before the full scope is known?

The first line has one job: to earn the next 30 seconds. It does that by letting the visitor recognise the page as the answer to their question. The rest of the pitch can follow.

## Write the landing page story before arranging the interface {#write-the-story-before-arranging-the-interface}

Page design finishes only when the content does. Every visual choice depends on what the offer means, which objection arrives next and what proof must sit near the claim. Placeholder copy postpones those choices and lets the layout become the strategy.

Draft a simple narrative first:

1. the situation the visitor recognises;
2. the change on offer;
3. the less obvious reason the problem persists;
4. the responsibility your organisation takes on;
5. the proof that makes that responsibility credible;
6. a proportionate next action.

Each section should add to the argument, so the proposition reads as one line of thought from top to bottom.

## Landing page design elements, in the order the reader needs them {#build-the-page-as-an-evidence-sequence}

Treat each section as a question the reader must be able to answer yes to.

| The reader thinks | The section's job | Useful proof |
|---|---|---|
| This is about my situation | Name the product situation and what is at stake | Real objects, roles or triggers from the visitor's product |
| This company sees the real problem | Show a diagnosis the visitor had not made | A failure pattern or a clear boundary |
| They can take useful responsibility | Say what they will take on and how you will work together | A map of who does what, a working model, a sample of the work |
| Their claims are credible | Put proof beside the claim | A moment from a case, an exact review, a result you can check |
| A conversation is worth the effort | Make the first step proportionate | A diagnostic, a short enquiry or a scoping question |

The usual feature-versus-benefit rule breaks down for a delivery partner. A buyer comparing partners often wants to know whether research reaches implementation. They ask whether the work includes building the interface in code, and how an AI feature will be evaluated. Those are service features, and for this buyer they are also the reasons to choose.

Ask instead what this visitor needs to believe next, and what would justify that belief.

## Keep the ad's promise from click to page to confirmation {#keep-the-promise-consistent-before-during-and-af}

The experience begins where the visitor arrived and ends only after the promised action is acknowledged. Search titles, advertisements, social posts, directories and referrals each set a promise and a level of detail. The page should pick that promise up in its first lines, before any company introduction.

Consistency is first a matter of words, then of looks. The same product situation, terminology and action should run through the page, form and confirmation. Visual continuity with the brand and product strengthens recognition, but a change in audience or offer has to be made in the words.

After the action, the confirmation should finish the same story: what arrived, what the visitor told you, what happens next and when. A visitor who described a detailed product situation should see it reflected on the confirmation screen.

When the landing experience belongs to a larger product, [Product Design](/services/product-design) makes the promise, the proof and the screens read as one story.

## What proof persuades a landing page visitor? {#use-proof-to-help-the-visitor-reason}

Logos show that a relationship existed. They say little about what was done. A testimonial shows how the collaboration felt, when it is exact, attributed and relevant to the nearby claim. A moment from a case, a sample of the work, or a clear account of scope and ownership usually does more than a distant wall of praise.

Constructive criticism can build trust when the response is visible. A review that names a real limitation and shows how the organisation handled it can be more informative than uniform approval. Leave the criticism in its original words, and publish only responses the record supports.

Video and motion pass the same test. Use them when seeing behaviour, hearing a person or following a sequence reduces uncertainty. Skip the decorative autoplay reel. Always provide captions, controls and a text equivalent for any information the video carries.

## Repeat one call to action, with a prompt for each stage {#one-action-can-appear-more-than-once}

A page can repeat one meaningful action without becoming repetitive. The first placement serves a buyer who arrived ready to act. A later placement serves someone who needed method or proof first.

Keep the destination and change the prompt around it:

- “Start a project” after the diagnosis.
- “Bring the workflow or roadmap decision” after the method.
- “Start with a short assessment” after fit guidance.

Avoid competing primary actions unless the page genuinely serves different buying stages. “Book a call”, “download a guide”, “subscribe”, “view pricing” and “start a trial” each belong to a different decision. One primary action can appear as several buttons, provided every prompt serves the same decision. Secondary links can answer real questions and stay secondary.

## What the confirmation should say after the form is sent {#the-confirmation-is-part-of-the-page}

After submission, tell the visitor:

- that the message arrived;
- what you kept from their enquiry;
- when a person will reply;
- what you may ask for next;
- how to correct or withdraw the enquiry.

Publish a reply time, such as “within two working days”, only once you can meet it consistently. Until then it is an operational claim you have yet to earn.

## High-converting landing page design: what to measure besides conversion rate {#measure-the-decision-not-the-decoration}

A conversion rate without traffic quality, intent and downstream fit can reward the wrong page. Pair the final action with signals from before and after it:

- qualified visits by source;
- progress to the first relevant proof;
- form starts and abandonment field by field;
- qualified submissions;
- sales acceptance;
- time to first human response;
- objections that keep recurring in conversations.

An A/B test can compare two versions of one defined hypothesis. When the versions differ in several unrelated ways, the result says little about what the buyer needed.

Testing has other limits. Low traffic makes small differences unreliable. A short-term click gain can lower lead quality. Seasonality, campaign mix or a change to form routing can contaminate the result. Write the hypothesis and the downstream success measure before choosing the variants.

## A landing page design example from our own site {#how-we-apply-this-on-our-own-site}

We hold our own website to the same rules. Every page on tcules.com ends in the same enquiry form, because each page serves one buyer's question and a second offer would split the visitor's attention between two choices.

What changes from page to page is the prompt above the form. It picks up the question that brought the visitor there. A case study about a crowded listing workflow asks which step in your workflow people keep working around. A case study about a specialist store asks what your buyers are trying to find.

The destination stays the same, and the words around it change with the visitor. It is the rule from the call-to-action section above, applied across a whole site.

## Five questions to answer before you design a landing page {#answer-these-five-questions-before-you-design-an}

Before designing a landing page, answer these five questions in plain text:

1. Which visitor situation does this page recognise?
2. What decision must the visitor make before taking action?
3. Which responsibility can the company state precisely?
4. What proof sits next to the largest claim?
5. What does the visitor expect immediately after the action?

If the answers are weak, another visual iteration will only make the uncertainty more polished. If the structure itself is unclear, start with [information architecture and workflow design](/services/product-design/information-architecture-workflow-design).

## Test the live landing page and form before launch {#how-should-the-live-page-and-form-be-validated}

A coherent page can still fail if it loads slowly, shifts during reading or responds poorly to interaction. Measure current Core Web Vitals with field data where available, and use lab checks to diagnose specific problems.

Test the action at each of these points: required-field explanation, invalid input, server failure, retry without lost work, successful submission and the promised confirmation. The confirmation must say what happened, what happens next and how the visitor can recover if the result is missing.

## Landing page design FAQs {#faq}

### What are landing page design best practices?

Decide the visitor, the offer and one primary outcome before any layout. Open with the promise that caused the visit, write the story before arranging the interface and put proof beside each claim. Then measure qualified submissions and sales acceptance alongside the conversion rate.

### What makes a B2B landing page convert?

A B2B landing page converts when the right visitor recognises their situation, understands the change on offer and can test the claims. Buyers need proof close to their own product, a clear account of what you take on and a first step sized to their stage. Generic praise rarely carries a considered purchase.

### How should a landing page match the ad that brought people in?

Pick up the ad's promise in the first lines, in the same words, before any company introduction. Keep the same product situation, terms and action through the page, the form and the confirmation. If the audience or offer changes, say so in words, because a matching look alone will confuse people.

### How should a landing page form be tested?

Test every state of the action: required-field explanations, invalid input, server failure, a retry that keeps the visitor's work, successful submission and the confirmation. Then watch form starts and abandonment field by field once the page is live. Check page speed with field data where you have it.

### How many calls to action should a landing page have?

One primary action, which can appear several times down the page with a different prompt each time. Secondary links can answer real questions and stay visually secondary. Competing offers such as a demo, a download and a trial on one page ask the visitor to make several choices at once.
