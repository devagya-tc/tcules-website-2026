*Case study · Benchmark Gensuite*

# Modernising a suite without making customers relearn it

Benchmark Gensuite was modernising the shared frontend of an established environment, health, safety, sustainability and operational-risk software suite. Tcules developed and tested shared interface patterns and a three-view scope selector in code, so modernisation decisions could be reviewed against existing customer habits before wider implementation.

The hard part was protecting how people already worked. Existing users had built fast habits on the interface as it stood, and a fresher look that broke those habits would have cost them more than it gave them.

As a member of Benchmark's technical leadership put it: "For established users, familiarity and consistency are important parts of efficient workflows."

## What had to change without making customers relearn the suite? {#the-opportunity-and-the-constraint-arrived-toget}
Prospective customers judged the suite against what enterprise software looks like now. Existing customers judged it against what they already knew how to do. Both judgements were fair, and they pulled in opposite directions.

The constraint was that an established suite cannot pause while it is redesigned. So the work started at the shared component layer, where a decision made once affects every application, and only then moved into one high-use feature where the decision could be tested against real work:

1. Stabilise. Resolve the reusable components and the states they have to carry.
2. Apply. Assemble those foundations inside an actual product workflow rather than a sample screen.
3. Evaluate. Separate the opportunities that are visual from the ones that are about how the interaction itself works.
4. Refine. Put investment where the research says the product and its users gain most.

The redesign reached into search, controls and navigation as well as appearance. That gave the team a way to improve how people worked across the suite.

## What the scope selector was actually being asked to do {#what-the-scope-selector-was-actually-being-asked}

The feature chosen for that test was the scope selector: the control by which a person tells the product which part of their organisation they are working in. Every application in the suite depends on it, and getting it wrong is expensive in a way that is easy to miss, because a person who selects the wrong scope does not see an error. They see the wrong data, presented confidently.

Three things were unclear in the existing control:

- Search. The boundary of a search was not explicit, and searching was not clearly distinguished from filtering, so a person could not always tell what a result set covered.
- Archive controls. The same control behaved differently in different areas of the suite.
- Custom group viewing. The role the mode was meant to play was not obvious to the people using it.

## Scope selection was three different needs in one interface {#scope-selection-was-three-different-needs-in-one}

The redesigned selector stopped treating scope selection as one task. It separated three genuinely different needs and gave each its own view, with the search boundary stated distinctly in each:

- Browse, for moving through the organisational hierarchy and choosing a scope deliberately.
- Recents and Favourites, for returning to the areas a person works in every day without navigating to them again.
- Nearby, for the field case, where location is a better way to find the right site than hierarchy is.

The third view is the one that shows what the work was for. Nearby is not a visual improvement. It is a different answer to the question of how a person finds their place in a large organisation, and it only becomes visible once the modernisation is treated as an interaction problem rather than a styling problem.

## We built the prototypes in code, so engineers could try them {#the-prototypes-were-code-not-pictures}

Tcules built the direction as working prototypes in HTML, CSS and JavaScript rather than as static designs. Two things follow from that choice. Interaction intent survives, because the prototype behaves rather than merely depicting behaviour. And feasibility enters the conversation while the decision is still open, because engineers can review the thing itself.

A Benchmark engineering stakeholder reviewing the work asked: "I was curious if you use an external library for drag and drop because Bootstrap 5 doesn't natively support it." The prototype brought that implementation dependency into the design review, while the team could still act on it.

The attachments prototype went further into the same territory, covering organisation, nested folders, previews, reordering and the states the system has to show while any of that is happening.

## From working prototypes to continuing delivery {#where-the-work-stands}

The prototypes let Benchmark's engineers check the design direction, and whether it could be built, before wider implementation. It produced a component direction for the Bootstrap 5.3 migration and confirmed the three-view approach to scope selection.

Benchmark had a shared component direction and working prototypes its engineers could inspect before broader implementation. The scope selector gave familiar work a clearer structure, with a separate path for people finding a site by location.

## What another suite owner can take from it {#what-another-suite-owner-can-take-from-it}

Before committing to a modernisation, separate four things that get bundled together: a framework upgrade, a visual refresh, a change to the interaction model, and a design-system programme. They have different costs, different risks and different people who have to agree to them. Deciding which of the four the product actually needs is what protects the workflows your existing customers have already learned.

The modernisation reasoning here is the substance of Tcules' [product modernisation](/services/product-modernisation) work. Proving direction in a running interface before production is [design engineering](/services/design-engineering), and the shared-component layer it started from is [design systems](/services/design-systems).
