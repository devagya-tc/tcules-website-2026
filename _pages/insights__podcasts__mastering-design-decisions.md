# Making product decisions across evidence and disciplines

Product decisions rarely arrive with one complete source of truth. Customer evidence may be partial. Product analytics can describe behaviour without explaining it. Engineering exposes feasibility and operating cost. Business leaders carry commitments and organisational consequence. Designers must make the user and future product legible inside that reality.

By Jatin Leuva · Published 16 July 2026 · Updated 8 September 2026

In this Tcules discussion, host Jatin Leuva, Founder and CEO of Tcules, speaks with Parasharan Chari, Founder of Medkart; Dhimant Trivedi, Head of Engineering at Contrado; and Akshay Vyas, Creative Head at Knovos. This article paraphrases the published recording rather than presenting a verbatim transcript.

[Watch the published recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=44Nta0CkcOY).

## The first decision is how to listen {#the-first-decision-is-how-to-listen}

The speakers describe several routes by which a product organisation can hear from customers: direct research, customer-service questions, QA feedback, product and stakeholder input, and observed product data.

Each route changes the evidence. A support ticket has already been framed by a problem severe enough to report. A product metric shows an event but not the person's motive. A stakeholder requirement may represent revenue, regulation, service or an individual request.

The practical lesson is to retain provenance:

- who observed the issue;
- which user and context it represents;
- whether it is direct or relayed;
- what happened, rather than the proposed feature;
- what evidence would challenge the interpretation.

## Data validates an interpretation; it does not explain itself {#data-validates-an-interpretation-it-does-not-exp}

One discussion concerns a feature that appears frequently in usage data. High use does not prove that the feature is valuable. People may be taking an indirect route because the intended one does not exist or is not understood.

Parasharan returns to it from the retail side and Dhimant from engineering: a metric names an event, and the factors that produced it still have to be investigated separately. This is a useful boundary between evidence and decision. Data can strengthen or weaken a hypothesis; it does not remove the need to understand the product situation.

## Medicine availability, as a worked example {#medicine-availability-as-a-worked-example}

The recording describes a pharmacy context with product availability distributed across a store, nearby stores, a warehouse and a much larger universe of medicines. A customer at the counter wants a dependable answer to one question: when can I get this medicine?

The product decision is not simply to expose more inventory data. The system must represent location, availability, fulfilment route and timing in a way the person at the counter can use. It is a useful example of why a product surface depends on an operating model.

## Roadmap decisions are negotiated constraints {#roadmap-decisions-are-negotiated-constraints}

The participants describe roadmap work as a recurring deliberation across requirement, value, feasibility, cost and time. Product managers may represent stakeholders; designers and engineers contribute different evidence.

A disagreement between the intended experience and current technical readiness does not have to end with one discipline winning. A responsible partial release can:

- meet a current business or user need;
- preserve the conceptual route to the intended product state;
- make compromise visible rather than encoding it as permanent truth;
- define what evidence or capability will trigger the next stage.

The dangerous version is a quick fix whose constraint disappears from organisational memory.

## Stakeholder disagreement is often missing translation {#stakeholder-disagreement-is-often-missing-transl}

The conversation challenges the easy explanation that a stakeholder is resisting design because of ego. A business leader may not understand the product consequence of a design proposal. A designer may not understand the operational or commercial consequence of their own proposal.

The bridge is not more persuasive presentation alone. It is a shared decision record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Objective | What change is the organisation trying to create? |
| User evidence | What is known about the affected work? |
| Business consequence | Revenue, cost, risk, service or strategic effect? |
| Technical constraint | What is infeasible, expensive or unsafe now? |
| Options | Which paths remain possible? |
| Accountable owner | Who makes the decision and carries the consequence? |
| Validation | What will be observed after commitment? |
| Deferred intent | What must not be lost from the future product state? |

Not every small choice needs the full record. Participation should scale with consequence.

[Product Design](/services/product-design) uses that record to carry an accepted decision into interaction and implementation.

## What makes a designer trustworthy inside an organisation {#what-makes-a-designer-trustworthy-inside-an-orga}

Near the end, the discussion identifies four areas a designer should understand: the product or service, the customer, company operations and the team's capability. The point is not that a designer must become expert in every department. It is that a proposal gains credibility when it can survive those realities.

The reciprocal obligation belongs to the organisation. A loosely framed need and inaccessible context make responsible design harder. Trust grows through work, interactions and demonstrated understanding, not through title alone.

## AI changes speed, not responsibility {#ai-changes-speed-not-responsibility}

The participants discuss AI as an accelerator whose usefulness depends on the practitioner's ability to frame and judge the work. The recording includes concerns about novices outsourcing fundamentals as well as examples of AI assisting coding, content, reporting and expression.

The durable point is neither enthusiasm nor refusal. Generation has become cheaper; the team still needs to understand requirements, evaluate output and own the effect on customers and operations.

Tcules applies the same responsibility model in [How We Work](/how-we-work) and [Product Design](/services/product-design).

## What should the team record when disciplines disagree? {#what-should-the-team-record-when-disciplines-dis}

When disciplines disagree, record the decision to be made, the evidence each interpretation relies on, the constraints that cannot move, the options rejected and the owner accountable for the next step. This does not remove negotiation; it makes the negotiation reviewable and prevents a roadmap choice from being rewritten later as an objective fact.

AI can produce more options and summaries faster, but responsibility remains with the people who understand the consequence and can challenge the framing. Keep the original evidence accessible, mark generated interpretation and record who accepted the resulting choice.
