# Inclusive product design for older and disabled people, from standard to release

By [Yash Bhatt](/insights/authors/yash-bhatt) · Published 2 May 2025 · Updated 26 October 2026 · 8 min read

Inclusive product design means software that older and disabled people can use with confidence, alongside everyone else. This guide gives you the standard to meet, a barrier-first way to plan, our position on assisted use, everyday examples and a release checklist.

Older people are many kinds of user, and age and disability are different things. People arrive at sixty with different abilities, confidence and experience of software, and they use whatever device and help is to hand.

Start with which demands of the product create a barrier for this person in this setting. Then remove or reduce that barrier without creating another. Done well, the result is dependable for the people who needed it and often easier for many others.

**Key takeaways**

- WCAG 2.2 gives a testable baseline, and research with people shows whether the product is actually usable.
- Plan from the barrier a person meets in their setting. A demographic label alone is too thin a reason for a design change.
- Assisted use involves two people, so it needs delegation, consent and a visible line of responsibility.
- Aim for clarity people can adapt: keep expert shortcuts and control, make errors specific and make recovery possible.
- Before release, check keyboard use, zoom and reflow, contrast, screen-reader behaviour and error recovery in real workflows.

## WCAG 2.2 for product teams: the accessibility baseline {#begin-with-standards-not-stereotypes}

[WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) gives testable success criteria. They cover content people can perceive, interfaces they can operate, information they can understand and code that works with assistive technologies. WCAG is a necessary baseline for web content and a useful reference for broader digital products.

The W3C also explains that accessibility requirements for people with disabilities overlap substantially with the needs of many older users. Age-related changes can affect vision, dexterity, hearing and cognition in different combinations. See [Web Accessibility and Older People](https://www.w3.org/WAI/older-users/).

The standard needs research on top of it. An automated check can pass while someone still cannot follow an account status, recover from an error or finish a task with their assistive technology.

## Inclusive design for accessibility: start from the barrier a person meets {#start-from-the-barrier-not-the-guideline}

| Need or situation | Barrier to look for | Response to try |
|---|---|---|
| Low vision or variable contrast sensitivity | Information carried only by low-contrast text or colour | Enough contrast, cues beyond colour, text and spacing people can adjust |
| Limited dexterity or tremor | Small targets, tight spacing, time pressure | Larger targets, alternatives, undo and adjustable time |
| Screen-reader use | Unlabelled controls, relationships that are only visual | Meaningful names, clear page structure and a tested reading order |
| Cognitive or memory load | Long instructions, hidden status, inconsistent terms | Plain content, visible progress, stable naming and steps people can undo |
| Hearing difference | Instructions or alerts that are audio only | Captions, transcripts and a visual alert that says the same |
| Occasional help from someone else | A supporter has to take over, with no way to record their authority | Delegation, consent, privacy and visible responsibility |

Use the table to plan. Testing with people tells you whether the response works.

## Our position: assisted use means two people on one account {#assisted-use-is-two-people-not-one}

The standards leave out one case: a daughter completing the form on her father's account, from her own device, with his consent and none of it recorded. Most products model one signed-in person. Assisted use involves two. The second needs a delegation, a consent record and a visible line of responsibility.

When the product cannot tell the two apart, the helper borrows a password, consent goes unrecorded and nobody can later say who changed what. Design the second person in from the start:

- an invitation from the account holder, with a clear scope of what the helper may see and do;
- a consent record the account holder can review and withdraw;
- an activity history that shows who acted, and on whose behalf;
- privacy for the parts of the account the helper has no reason to see;
- a way for the helper to step back without losing work in progress.

We treat this as a product model question to work out early, because a delegation added after release rarely reaches every screen.

## Principles of inclusive design: clarity people can adapt {#do-not-simplify-the-person-away}

“Simple” can mean removing useful control. A product that hides detail can become harder for an experienced older user, or for a disabled professional who needs quick access to advanced work.

Aim for clarity that people can adapt:

- use consistent language and structure;
- let text enlarge and reflow without losing function;
- show status and consequences before an action;
- make errors specific and recovery possible;
- support keyboard and assistive-technology paths;
- keep expert shortcuts, as long as they stay accessible;
- avoid automatic motion and time limits that people cannot control.

## Inclusive design examples from everyday products {#inclusive-design-examples}

Most inclusive design is unglamorous. Each example below removes a barrier for older or disabled people and makes the product easier for someone else as well.

| Situation | Inclusive design example | Who else it helps |
|---|---|---|
| A timed checkout or session | A warning before time runs out, with one action to extend | Anyone interrupted mid-task |
| A status shown in colour | A text label beside the colour, such as "Overdue" | People in bright light or on a dim screen |
| A form that fails validation | The error named beside the field, with every entry kept | Anyone filling in a long form |
| A video walkthrough | Captions and a transcript | People watching without sound |
| A dense settings screen | Text that enlarges and reflows with every control still in reach | People on small or split screens |
| Help from a family member | Invited access with a set scope and a record of who acted | Teams that share an account |

## Designing for older adults: who takes part in your research {#include-people-in-the-evidence}

Recruit people who reflect the product's real audience and the access needs that go with it. Plan for:

- accessible consent and scheduling;
- compatibility with participants' devices and assistive technology;
- extra time, offered as normal;
- remote and in-person options;
- fair payment for participants;
- privacy when a supporter takes part;
- reports that trace each barrier to the product or setting that caused it.

Ask how the person completes the work today, what support they rely on and where their surroundings add to the barrier. A lab task alone can miss glare, noise, fatigue, shared devices and unstable connections.

## Tie each design change to a barrier you observed {#connect-the-response-to-a-real-barrier}

Begin with the person, their situation and their goal. Identify the access need and the barrier that the product or its surroundings create. Then check any proposed response against a relevant standard and against what participants were seen doing.

Each design change needs an observed barrier behind it. A demographic label on its own is too thin a reason. [Product Design](/services/product-design) can carry that barrier from research and interaction into how the product behaves.

## Inclusive design checklist before release {#confirm-these-before-you-release}

Before release, confirm:

- page structure and accessible names that assistive technology can read;
- keyboard operation and visible focus;
- zoom, reflow and text spacing;
- contrast, and meaning carried by more than colour;
- target size and alternative input;
- error messages that identify the problem, and a way to recover;
- captions, transcripts and alternatives;
- screen-reader behaviour in representative workflows;
- research with people, where standards cannot show whether the product is usable.

Build accessibility into your [design system](/services/design-systems) and your research plan from the start, well before the final checklist.

## What WCAG conformance proves, and what research adds {#what-does-conformance-prove}

WCAG conformance shows that a product meets testable requirements. Usability takes three layers. Automated tools catch a subset of failures. Knowledgeable human review interprets the rest of the interaction. People who use the product confirm that the work gets done.

Involve disabled and older participants early enough to change the product model, as well as at final validation. Define release scenarios by barrier and task: keyboard-only operation, screen-reader sequence, magnification and reflow, reduced dexterity, memory or comprehension load, error recovery and time pressure. Record what was automated, what was reviewed manually, who took part and which claims the release evidence does not support.

## Inclusive product design FAQs {#faq}

### How do I design a product for older and disabled people?

Start from the barrier a person meets in their setting, then remove it without creating another. Use WCAG 2.2 as the testable baseline, and research with older and disabled people to show whether the product is actually usable. Before release, check keyboard use, zoom, contrast, screen readers and error recovery in real workflows.

### Is WCAG compliance enough for inclusive design?

WCAG compliance is the baseline, and inclusive design asks for more. Automated checks catch a subset of failures, expert review interprets the rest, and people who use the product confirm the work gets done. Plan all three layers and record which claims each one supports.

### What are the principles of inclusive design?

Plan from the barrier a person meets, keep clarity adaptable and involve the people affected in research. Tie every design change to an observed barrier, and check that removing one barrier creates no new one. Keep expert shortcuts and control, so a simpler screen costs nobody their speed.

### How does age change how people use software?

Age-related changes can affect vision, dexterity, hearing and cognition, in different combinations for different people. Experience and confidence with software vary just as widely, so age alone predicts little. Design for the barrier, such as small targets or low contrast, and test with older participants on their own devices.

### What makes a website easier for older adults to use?

Text that enlarges and reflows without breaking the page, strong contrast, large targets and consistent wording do most of the work. Show status before an action, make error messages specific and remove time limits people cannot control. Each of these helps younger visitors too.
