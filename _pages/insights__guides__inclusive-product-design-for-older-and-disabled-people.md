# Inclusive product design for older and disabled people

Inclusive product design combines a testable accessibility baseline with direct involvement from people who encounter barriers. Older people are not one user type, and age is not a synonym for disability. People arrive at sixty with different abilities, different confidence and different experience of software, and they use whatever device and whatever help is to hand.

By Yash Bhatt · Published 2 May 2025 · Updated 8 September 2026

The practical question is: which product demands create a barrier for this person in this context, and how can the product remove or reduce it without making a different need worse?

## Begin with standards, not stereotypes {#begin-with-standards-not-stereotypes}

[WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) provides testable success criteria across perceivable content, operable interfaces, understandable information and implementation compatible with a wide range of user agents and assistive technologies. It is a necessary baseline for web content and a useful reference for broader digital products.

The W3C also explains that accessibility requirements for people with disabilities overlap substantially with the needs of many older users, while age-related changes can affect vision, dexterity, hearing and cognition in different combinations. See [Web Accessibility and Older People](https://www.w3.org/WAI/older-users/).

Standards do not remove the need for research. Passing an automated check does not show whether a person can understand an account state, recover from an error or complete a workflow with the assistive technology they use.

## Start from the barrier, not the guideline {#start-from-the-barrier-not-the-guideline}

| Need or context | Barrier to investigate | Product response to evaluate |
|---|---|---|
| Low vision or variable contrast sensitivity | Information conveyed by low-contrast text or colour alone | Sufficient contrast, non-colour cues, adaptable text and spacing |
| Limited dexterity or tremor | Small targets, tight spacing, time pressure | Adequate targets, alternatives, undo and adjustable time |
| Screen-reader use | Unlabelled controls or visual-only relationships | Semantic names, programmatic structure and tested reading order |
| Cognitive or memory demand | Long instructions, hidden state, inconsistent terms | Plain content, visible progress, stable naming and recoverable steps |
| Hearing difference | Audio-only instruction or alert | Captions, transcripts and equivalent visual notification |
| Intermittent assistance | A supporter must take over without an authority model | Delegation, consent, privacy and visible responsibility |

The matrix is a planning aid, not a substitute for testing with people.

### Assisted use is two people, not one {#assisted-use-is-two-people-not-one}

The case standards do not cover: a daughter completing the form on her father's account, from her own device, with his consent and none of it recorded. Products model one authenticated person. Assisted use is two, and the second one needs a delegation, a consent record and a visible line of responsibility.

## Do not simplify the person away {#do-not-simplify-the-person-away}

“Simple” can mean removing useful control. A product that hides detail may become harder for an experienced older user or a disabled professional who needs efficient access to advanced work.

Prefer adaptable clarity:

- maintain consistent language and structure;
- allow enlargement and reflow without loss of function;
- expose status and consequence before action;
- make errors specific and recovery possible;
- support keyboard and assistive-technology paths;
- preserve expert shortcuts where they remain accessible;
- avoid automatic motion and time limits that cannot be controlled.

## Include people in the evidence {#include-people-in-the-evidence}

Recruitment should reflect the product's real audience and relevant access needs. Plan for:

- accessible consent and scheduling;
- compatibility with participants' devices and assistive technology;
- additional time without treating it as a deficit;
- remote and in-person alternatives;
- compensation;
- privacy when a supporter participates;
- reporting that describes barriers rather than blaming the participant.

Ask how the person currently completes the work, what support they rely on and where the environment contributes to the barrier. A lab task alone may miss the reality of glare, noise, fatigue, shared devices or unstable connectivity.

## Connect the response to a real barrier {#connect-the-response-to-a-real-barrier}

Begin with the person, context and goal; identify the access need and the barrier created by the product or environment. A proposed response must address that barrier and be checked against both a relevant standard and observed participant evidence. A demographic label alone cannot justify a design prescription.

[Product Design](/services/product-design) and [Frontend Development](/services/software-development/frontend-development) can carry that barrier from research and interaction into implemented behaviour.

## Confirm these before you release {#confirm-these-before-you-release}

Before release, confirm:

- semantic structure and accessible names;
- keyboard operation and visible focus;
- reflow, zoom and text spacing;
- contrast and non-colour communication;
- target size and alternative input;
- error identification and recovery;
- captions, transcripts and alternatives;
- screen-reader behaviour in representative workflows;
- research with people where standards cannot establish usability.

For product work, [UX research](/services/product-design/ux-research) and [Design Systems](/services/design-systems) can make accessibility part of decisions and implementation rather than a final checklist.

## What does conformance prove? {#what-does-conformance-prove}

WCAG provides testable requirements, automated tools catch a subset of failures, and knowledgeable human evaluation interprets the remaining interaction. None of these alone proves that a workflow is usable. Include disabled and older participants early enough to change the product model, not only at final validation.

Define release scenarios by barrier and task: keyboard-only operation, screen-reader sequence, magnification and reflow, reduced dexterity, memory or comprehension load, error recovery and time pressure. Record what was automated, what was reviewed manually, who participated and which unsupported claims remain outside the release evidence.
