05

Imagery

Three kinds of image, one set of rules. Client work is shown as shipped: cut out and placed on our ground, or flat where the whole layout is the subject. People are photographed in the light that is there, straight on, on a phone. Illustration is line-led, built on a stated concept, and used where there is no screen to show, and as a project's cover and banner where the product's own UI cannot carry the concept (§9a). Every rule is checkable by looking at the image.

Colour is referenced by token name; the values live in chapter 02. Type, layout and component behaviour are inherited from the design system and are not restated. There is no dark mode on the site, so no photograph or capture needs a second variant. Ink and blue are reserved surfaces, on the site and in social (chapter 10 §A4), so an illustration is made for paper and paper-deep first; one placed on ink is recoloured per §4a. Photographs marked stand-in are credited Unsplash images, which this internal document may carry and a public surface may not (§10).

1 · Purpose, categories, roles

What imagery does

Imagery is evidence. It shows the work as it shipped, the people who made it, and the ideas that have no screen yet. It does not decorate, set a mood or fill a space.

Client work and mockups

Screenshots, cut-out mockups and framed screens of shipped or unshipped work, always with written client approval. Highest volume. Leads on the website, case studies, proposals and decks. Produced to suit the product, not our site (§5).

Team and studio

People at work in the studio, and the studio itself. Shot on phones, in available light. Leads on About, hiring, bylines, and the content of LinkedIn and Clutch profiles; a profile's own banner and avatar follow chapter 10 §D1.

Illustration

Line-led artwork for a concept, service or process that has no screen to show. Built on a concept and generated from a brief (§4, §10). Leads on articles, blog posts, service pages and deck dividers, and is a project's cover and banner wherever the product's own UI cannot carry the concept (§9a). Never as evidence on a case study.

Decorative or non-decorative

An image is non-decorative if removing it removes information the text beside it does not carry. Test: cover the image and read the page. If something is now missing, it is non-decorative and takes a caption and alt text (a project cover or banner takes no caption, §9a) (§7). If nothing is missing, it is decorative and takes empty alt and no caption. The call is made by whoever places the image and is recorded in the CMS alt field.

When illustration substitutes for photography

Only when the subject has no screen and no person: a method, a service line, an abstraction, an article's idea. Illustration never substitutes for a screenshot that exists, except as a project cover or banner (§9a) or a case-study social cover authored for its channel (chapter 10), and never depicts a person in place of a team photograph.

What this chapter owns

Photographs, screenshots, framed screens and illustration, including how they are exported and placed. It does not own icons (chapter 04, up to 64px and a single glyph), charts (chapter 09), moving image (chapter 08) or composed graphics that carry type: posts, carousels, share previews and carousel covers (chapter 10). An image placed inside one of those as a plate follows this chapter. A still frame taken from motion is an image and follows this chapter.

Which category leads on which surface

Every surface, not only the website. Where two are named, the first leads and the second supports.

1a · What imagery conveys

What an image says about the people in it

The sections that follow decide whether an image is accurate. This one decides what it implies, which is not the same test. One question is asked of every image before it is placed: what does this say about the people in it — the person using the work, the client, us? Five answers are permitted.

The person using the work

ConveysCapable use. The user is in command of the thing in front of them: reading it, deciding, getting the task done. Where the subject is difficulty, the difficulty is shown in the system — the screen, the flow, the step count, the data — and never on the person. A capture annotated at the point of friction (§2) carries a usability finding; a face cannot.
Fails ifFrustration, confusion, fatigue or defeat is legible in a person: head in hands, squinting, jaw set, phone held at arm's length. The composition or the caption makes the user the cause of the problem. A before state is illustrated by an unhappy person rather than by the interface that produced the unhappiness.

The client and their existing product

ConveysA before state is permitted, labelled as a before and dated, shown as a state of the product under the constraints it was built in. The change is described as a change in the system — a flow, a hierarchy, a model — and the client's team is a party to it.
Fails ifThe before is undated or unlabelled, or cropped or captured to look worse than it was. The framing reads as a rescue, a clean-up or a mess we inherited. The client's own brand is made to look cheap. A named client's before appears without the approval §10 requires.

The two states above are redrawn at one scale to show what a pair has to do. A published pair is two cut-outs of the same part, or two whole-layout captures, each at 2× or better (§2).

Us, and seniority

ConveysPeople doing the work at the scale they do it: a screen half-built, a board mid-argument, two people at one monitor. Sessions are documented as sessions — where someone is standing and presenting to a seated room, that is the event, the room is as full as it was, and the caption names it. Seniority is not staged: nobody is arranged above or in front of a group for the camera, and founders are photographed working like everyone else. No single face stands in for the studio.
Fails ifThe standing-and-seated arrangement is set up for the camera, or used as a portrait of authority with no session to name. Arms folded in front of a team. A lone silhouette at a window. One person on more than a third of the people images on a surface. Portrait framing, dress or grooming that differs by seniority.

Results and proof

ConveysAn image proves only what it shows. A chart inside a capture is the product's chart as it ran, and the caption says whose data it is and over what period (§7). A result we claim is claimed in words beside the image, with the measure and the period, rather than implied by the picture.
Fails ifA capture is selected or re-run so the line rises. Unlabelled axes are presented as evidence. Demo or replaced data (§2) is implied to be live. A figure in the image cannot be sourced from the caption. A metric is drawn into an illustration (§4).

Competitors and other studios

ConveysThey do not appear. Our work is shown against the product we were given, or against nothing.
Fails ifA competitor's product, mark or screen appears anywhere in an image. A side-by-side invites a comparison. Another studio's work is shown as a before without its authorship stated and the client's approval. A badge, ranking or logo places us above a named party.
2 · Client work and mockups

Flat, as shipped, chrome stripped

A screenshot is a record. The rules below make it one that can be checked against the live product. Client-work assets are produced in the client product's own palette; nothing of ours is added around a capture (§5).

RulePass condition
What may be shownShipped or unshipped work, always with written client approval (§10). Final UI only. Wireframes, sketches and prototypes appear only inside a process section and are captioned as such.
FidelityCaptured at device pixel ratio 2 or higher from the running product or the design file. No upscaled captures; no JPEG artefacts; type in the capture is crisp at 100% on a 2560px export.
Device framesOn the website: none. Figure and PageHero media render the capture with a hairline rule and --radius-0; that rule is the frame. In decks and proposals only, a frame is permitted as a plain rectangle with the device's own corner radius, one stroke, no hardware detail, no manufacturer marks. Never: photoreal device renders, hands holding phones, laptops on desks, 3D renders. The permitted frame is a capture's: a cut-out mockup takes no frame at any size, because its silhouette is its edge (§2a).
Browser chromeStripped. The capture starts at the page's top edge. One exception: when the address bar is the subject (a URL structure, a favicon), it stays and the caption says so.
ProjectionFlat only. No perspective, no isometric, no rotation, no skew. Test: every edge of the screen is parallel to a canvas edge. (Isometric projection belongs to illustration, §4a, and never to a real screen.)
ElevationA flat capture sits flat: --img-frame-elevation = 0. Its one edge cue is the placement's hairline rule, so it takes no shadow. Behind it, the product's own ground colour taken from its UI and nothing else: no gradient, no photograph, no pattern. A cut-out has no page edge of its own: it sits on the placement's own ground, or on the contrast panel where its edge does not hold, and may carry one shadow, --img-crop-elevation = 0 0 12px rgba(27, 27, 24, .08) at the 2560 export — no offset, tight spread, 8% ink — never stacked, never coloured, never with perspective (§2a).
Content in frameReal content as shipped. Where content is confidential it is replaced, not obscured: fictional values of the same type and length in the same place (see redaction). No lorem ipsum, no grey blocks.
AnnotationsOn a capture or a cut-out, outside a project cover (§9a). Numbered rings: --annot-ring = 56px, --annot-stroke = 3px on the working canvas, number in mono. One filled --color-brandeis-500 ring with a --color-white numeral for the point that matters; --color-neutral-900 outline rings for the observations around it. Where the product’s own UI is blue at that spot, the filled ring takes a --color-paper hairline outside its stroke so it stays separable. Maximum three per image, legend in the caption, not on the image. No arrows, no callout boxes, no fills other than the one ring. On a composed graphic the legend goes in the image box's caption.
What the image isA part of the UI, not a whole screen, shown as a cut-out (§2a) at every size: one component, one state or one moment — a table with its filters, a summary card, one step of a flow — at 100% pixel scale or larger, and the caption says which part it is. At small sizes the check is the type floor in §2a rather than a pixel ratio. A whole screen appears only as a flat capture at full column, where the layout itself is the subject, and the caption says so. Below full-width, a whole screen loses the detail that made it evidence: at card, thumbnail and social sizes the part is the only thing a reader can actually see.
The pick testName what a reader takes from the image, in one phrase: a chart with a quarter-on-quarter change, a count sourced to the reviews it came from. If the phrase needs a second element to make sense, or if it comes out as a dashboard, some cards or a list, the crop is wrong — crop tighter, or pick another part of the product. One element carries the image; whatever else is in frame is context around it, never a second subject.
Never degraded to fitWhere a capture will not read at its placement, the crop changes and the content does not. Blurring the small type, greeking it, deleting it, redrawing the fragment with loosened spacing and enlarged type, or rebuilding it as a simplified vignette: the first three are the methods redaction below refuses, and the last two are a re-typed screen under misrepresentation. Type inside a capture that falls below legibility stays as it is, because it is content. What changes is how much of the screen is in frame, and at small sizes how few parts are in the composition (§2a).
Crop disciplineA crop lands on a UI element's own edge or outside it, never through one, and never through type. Where the whole screen is the subject it is full viewport width, cropped on the block axis only. Captures only: a cut-out mockup has no crop on three sides and may bleed deliberately off one or two edges (§2a).
Multi-screenFor captures: two or three whole screens per composition, never more. A cut-out counts parts instead (§2a). Same scale, same projection, top edges aligned. The gap between screens is one value within a composition and is set per asset, since the asset is produced to the product (§5); it is not a brand token.
Before and afterA pair is one comparison: the same region of the product, at the same apparent scale, cropped to the same ratio, so the change is the only difference between the two images. Different crops, different zoom levels or two different screens make a pair nobody can read. Each is labelled and dated (§1a).
RedactionMethod: replace. Names, emails, figures and addresses are swapped for fictional equivalents before capture, or in the design file. Blur, pixelation and black bars are not permitted in a published image; they read as censorship and are reversible. --img-redact-method = replace.
MisrepresentationFails if any of: a feature is shown that did not ship; a screen is re-skinned or re-typed after the fact; client branding is removed without the client asking; a mobile screen is presented as desktop or the reverse; concept work is shown without "Concept" in the caption; work by another party is shown as ours.

The same screen at card width

Three treatments of one screen, each in the same 3:2 card box at the width a thumbnail or a feed post actually gives it. The progression is screen, panel, cut-out element, and it is not a matter of taste: the treatment is decided by the size the image will be read at, and it tightens until the thing the image is evidence for can be read. The third is the method §2a sets out.

Checked at three sizes

An asset is approved at the size it will be read at, not the size it was made at. Two checks, both on the final asset. At 270px wide the element is nameable within a couple of seconds — the pick test, run at the width a feed gives it. At 120px wide the image is still one recognisable shape rather than a field of grey. The third treatment above passes both; the first two fail the first. And every image placed on the website is checked at 326px wide, the content column in a 390px viewport: one asset serves every width, so an image that does not survive the mobile column is not used, however it looks on desktop. No image may depend on a mobile-only crop to pass (§8).

Where imagery of client work may go

Client work is evidence, so it goes where evidence is what the surface is for, and at every size that evidence is a cut-out (§2a). A flat capture is kept for the one case a cut-out cannot carry: a whole layout that is itself the subject. Three placements take neither, and each has something better to carry: an illustration, a photograph, or the words alone.

2a · Client work and mockups · cut-outs

Cut out, assembled, read at 300px

The default for client work at every size, because every image is also read at the 326px mobile column (§2). A rectangular capture stops working somewhere under 600px. The crop that keeps one element legible has by then lost what the element is for, and the crop that keeps the context legible has lost the words. The method below is what they take instead: the product's own UI, lifted out of its page, reduced to the few parts that carry the point, and placed straight onto the surface's ground with no frame of any kind. It is a mockup rather than a record, so the rules exist to keep it as checkable as a capture.

RulePass condition
When it is usedEvery placement of client work: Figure and PageHero media, BlockCard and ListCard media, feed posts, carousel inner slides, share previews, video thumbnails, slides. The one exception is a whole layout that is itself the subject, which is a flat capture at full column (§2).
What it is made ofThe product's own UI at export fidelity, from the running product or its design file. Parts may be moved and panels may be truncated. Nothing is redrawn: type, weight, spacing inside a part, radii, iconography and colour stay as the product renders them.
Cut out, not framedNo page edge, no window, no device. Mastered as PNG with alpha and served as WebP with alpha (§8), so the placement's own ground shows through, and the silhouette is the frame. Where the product's UI does not hold its edge against that ground — below 3:1 between the part's outer edge and the adjacent ground — the cut-out sits on a flat panel of --color-paper, --color-paper-deep or --color-neutral-950 filling the slot: no hairline, no radius, no second shadow on the panel. The panel is chosen for contrast; if the edge holds, there is no panel. Repainting the product to match our ground is never permitted.
How much is in frameOne working part, plus what it connects to and what acts on it. Up to five parts at full column, up to three on a single feed post, one per carousel slide, two in a BlockCard, one in a ListCard thumbnail. A dialog or panel that carries its own edge counts as one part and may be shown whole, which is the method's upper bound; beyond it the image is a screen again, and a screen does not read here. At card width a panel is a screen (§2).
Scale floorScale to the type, not to the part. At the placement's width the mockup's leading label reads at 15px or more and its smallest label at 11px or more, measured on the final asset. Where the parts cannot all clear the floor, parts come out of the composition — nothing is scaled down to fit (§2).
BleedA part may run off one or two canvas edges to say it continues past the frame. Never all four, never through type, and the bleed edge lands in a part's own empty area rather than through a control.
OverhangA part sits outside its container's edge where the product puts it there — an open dropdown, a popover, a floating toolbar — or where a control the container owns is lifted whole to sit across its edge, as the step row does above. An overhang stays intact, overlaps ground rather than other content, and never changes what the control says. Nothing else is nudged out of a container for effect.
ConnectorsThe product's own, as it draws them: hairline, right-angled, with its endpoint dots. We add no arrows, curves, labels or highlights of our own; annotation stays in the caption (§2).
Colour and elevationThe product's palette. One accent carries the part; a second appears only where the product itself uses it to mark a different kind of content, as the sourced-category card does above. Everything else stays neutral. Third-party marks stay in their own colour, because they are content. A drop shadow is permitted behind a cut-out: --img-crop-elevation, one per part, never stacked, never coloured, never with perspective, and none where the product draws its own.
Copy stays as shippedThe product's own words, including its own mistakes: the category card above reads House hold cleaning supplies and stays that way. Correcting a client's copy inside a mockup is a re-typed screen under misrepresentation (§2). Confidential values are still replaced rather than corrected (§2 redaction).
CaptionNone on a project cover or banner (§9a). Everywhere else it names the product, names what the mockup shows, and says composed from the live product where parts have been moved. A composed mockup is not a record of a whole screen and cannot stand as evidence of a layout. It can stand as a before and after when both halves are cut from the same part of the same product, at one scale, and the caption says so (§2). On social the set's eyebrow names the client, and the image-box caption names the part (§7).
Fails ifParts from two products in one composition; a state assembled that the product cannot reach; type or spacing redrawn to fit; perspective, rotation or a device frame; a drawn callout, arrow or glow; more than one shadow on a part; a UI kit part standing in for the client's.

The same mockup at card width

Both compositions above, each placed in the same 270 × 180 box, the width a feed card gives it. The wide one keeps its four parts and loses its labels; the tight one keeps two parts at the scale they were composed at and bleeds off the bottom edge. Fewer parts, not a smaller mockup — the same two checks as a capture apply, nameable at 270px and one recognisable shape at 120px.

3 · Team and studio photography

People at work, in the light that is there

Shot on phones, by the team, in the studio. The art direction is decided (§3a); these rules hold with it.

RulePass condition
Subject matterPeople working, talking, sketching, reviewing; the studio's rooms, tools and surfaces. Never appears: legible client work on any screen or wall; client names on boards or documents; anyone who has not consented; children; alcohol; other companies' branding as the subject.
CameraThe phone's main camera at 1× or 2×. No ultrawide, no digital zoom past 2×, no filters, no live effects. Portrait mode (synthetic background blur): off. The blur edge halos at 2560px.
Portrait framingHead and shoulders, subject facing camera, eye line in the upper third of the frame. Headroom no more than 10% of frame height above the top of the head. Shot square at capture, or composed for a square crop, and the placement keeps 1:1 everywhere: website, social and decks. Never letterboxed (chapter 10 §B2).
Environmental framingSubject occupies 25–50% of frame width; the room is legible around them. Eye line off camera. Camera at the subject's eye height; horizon and verticals level (test: door frames and screen edges are parallel to the canvas edges).
Direction on a shootDirected candid. Subjects are placed, then asked to work; no eye contact with the camera except in portraits; no pointing at screens, no staged laughter, no handshakes.
BackgroundsThe studio only: a plain wall or the working environment. No seamless paper, no branded backdrop, no green screen, no outdoor location shoots. Client work on background screens is blanked before the shot, not after.
Two shoots, one brandA new set passes if it matches the last on all of: available light with white balance to the dominant source (§3a), 1× or 2× main camera, straight-on level framing, no grain, full tonal range, no treatment, and this table. Because light varies by hour and room, the tonal rule (neutral greys, no clipping) is what makes two sets match; check it on every select.
Individual v groupIndividual for profiles, bylines and quotes. Group for About and careers, up to six recognisable faces, every one consented. Larger groups are shot so faces beyond six are not recognisable (turned away, out of focus).
Consent and reviewWritten consent before publishing, scoped to the surfaces named in §1. Subject reviews and approves the selects before first publication. Opt-out at any time, honoured on every surface we control at the next deploy. On leaving Tcules, a person's images are withdrawn from active surfaces at the next deploy unless they opt in to remain.
Portrait · stand-in. Facing camera, eye line in the upper third, available light, shot square and never masked (§6).
Environmental · two people at one screen, the room legible around them, no eye contact with the camera.
Studio · the wall mid-project. Available light, nothing legible that belongs to a client.

4 · Illustration

Where there is no screen

An illustration is not decoration and not a mood. It is a concept, drawn: a construction whose arrangement carries one message, which a reader recovers by looking at it. An illustration that cannot be read back to a message is decoration, and decoration has no placement (§1).

RulePass condition
The messageEvery illustration has one line, written before it is made: what this illustration says. One sentence, present tense, about the subject and not about the illustration — "the intent set in the first decision is still there in what ships", "a component crosses from design to build without being translated". The line is filed with the asset.
Read-backWhoever places the illustration says what it shows, without the line in front of them, and the two match. If the illustration carries several readings, or none, it is remade. An illustration that passes every other rule and fails this one does not ship.
Structure carries itThe message is carried by arrangement: what sits on what, what is aligned, what is repeated, what is open, what is marked (§4a). Not by a label, and not by a metaphor the reader has to be told. Cover the labels and the illustration still says most of it.
When usedThe subject is a concept, method, service, process or article idea with no shipped screen to show. Test: if a real screenshot could stand in this position, use it and the illustration fails. Two exceptions: a project's cover and banner, which convey the concept, and a case-study social cover authored for its channel (chapter 10). For the cover and banner, §9a decides which kind does it.
When not usedNever as a substitute for evidence: where client work is the subject, a real capture must stand, so the body of a case-study page and the image boxes inside a case-study social set carry cut-outs. A project's cover and banner convey its concept, as a product-UI cut-out or an illustration (§9a). A case-study social cover is authored for its channel and may carry an illustration that diagrams the problem, provided the set still shows the product in an image box (chapter 10 §A7). Never to depict a person in place of a team photograph. Never as a decorative background (§6).
Detail relative to placementChecked at the smallest size it will appear at: a 3:2 BlockCard image at card width, or a 160px ListCard thumbnail, and at the 326px mobile column. Line weight holds at any size (§4a), so what is checked is detail — no two objects merging, and the message still arriving. Where it does not, simplify: fewer elements, not smaller ones — a few bars and blocks in place of dense UI, larger dots at a wider pitch. Never ship a different illustration at the smaller size.
Illustration v iconAn icon is one Solar Bold glyph at 64px or below, in the current ink (chapter 04). Anything generated or drawn by us, anything above 64px, or any composition of more than one element is illustration and follows this chapter. Icons are never enlarged into illustrations; illustrations are never shrunk into icons.
Never illustratedClient logos or products; real, identifiable people; competitors' products; awards and badges; anything a photograph or screenshot already exists for, except a project cover or banner chosen by §9a. Human figures: none. A person appears as their place — a seat, a station, a marked tile — never as a body; people are photographed (§3).
MethodGenerated from a written brief (§10) answered in the order of §4d, then corrected by hand until it meets §4a row by row. Generation artefacts are fixed rather than tolerated: broken lines, doubled edges, axes that are nearly but not quite parallel, stray marks, pseudo-text.
FormatSVG, and the SVG is the asset. It is placed as SVG wherever the surface renders SVG. Where an asset is flattened later — a social export, a deck, a PDF, a raster thumbnail — it is exported from the SVG at that asset's size, and the SVG stays filed with it (§8).
Text and numbersNo prose inside the image. Annotation labels that name parts of the illustration are permitted per §4b. A number that is only information — a date, a price, a metric, a count in a stat line — is never illustrated and never set inside the illustration; it is type in the layout around it (chapter 10). A number appears inside an illustration only where the quantity itself is what the illustration is about, and then it is constructed on the illustration’s own axes as counted or measured form, not set as type. Generated pseudo-text (glyph-like marks that spell nothing) fails the image.
4a · Illustration · Direction

Isometric line

Flat planes in parallel projection, drawn in one line weight. Blue draws the system; peridot marks the one item that travels through it (§4b). Lines over surfaces, on paper and on ink.

RulePass condition
ProjectionParallel. Each horizontal axis runs two units across to one unit down (26.57°); verticals stay vertical. No vanishing point, no perspective, no rotation off those axes. Test: any two edges parallel in the object are parallel in the illustration and measure the same slope.
Placed by formulaEvery point is placed in flat plane coordinates and projected; nothing is nudged by eye, so edges on different objects stay parallel. For a point (x, y) at height z, on a plane whose left corner is (ox, oy): sx = ox + (x + y) × 0.7071 and sy = oy + (y − x) × 0.3536 − z × 0.8660.
ConstructionBuilt, not sketched. Planes are flat: a face with an outline and no thickness, standing panels included. Three forms show thickness: the plinth, a low raised block that lifts an object off its plane (§4b), kept low; the carried item; and a cylinder that is itself the form. A volume's side faces follow one light, from the upper right, so the left-facing side is always the darker of the two. Depth comes from stacking and position, not from edges. No freehand line, no wobble, no sketch texture.
ElevationObjects that share a ground share an elevation, and their bases meet it; nothing floats unless floating is the point. Where elevation does differ, the difference carries meaning — a stack of layers, one object lifted on a plinth — and every object's base sits on a plane that exists in the illustration. Test: name the plane each object stands on. If an object has none, it is misdrawn, not stylised.
Size and stackingMain planes are 400–640 units wide on the 1000-unit source canvas. Stacked planes sit at equal vertical spacing. A point on a lower plane is hidden where y − x is less than 400 − 2.45 × the gap (in z units), so echoes on lower planes are placed well clear of that line, toward the front corner.
OcclusionObjects are opaque. A nearer object hides what is behind it, using the plane face in the ground's own colour. What is behind shows through in two cases, and the illustration makes clear which: the nearer object is unbuilt or proposed, so it is dashed, with no fill or at most an echo tint; or the message is the relationship between front and back — continuity across layers, a thing inside a container, a cutaway — so the nearer object is outline-only throughout. Mixed treatment within one class of object fails: six seats are all opaque or all open, never four and two.
Line v fillLine-led. Every object is drawn in outline. Fills are the plane face, the one solid blue plane, the carried item and its echoes (§4b), and a plinth's side faces. Fails if filled area exceeds line-bounded open area.
Line weightOne weight, with three named exceptions. --ill-stroke = 1.5px for every outline and all UI detail, round joins and caps, held constant on screen at every size: vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke on every stroked path. Dashed and dotted lines carry the same weight. The exceptions are ratios of it and are non-scaling too: the ink seam at 1.75× (2.6px), a peridot outline on the blue plane or on ink at 1.25× (1.9px), and the carried item's path at 5× (7.5px). Test: open the file at 320px and at 2560px; the line measures the same in both, and the illustration reads in both.
Dashes--ill-dash = 6 4 for every dashed outline: a ground, a boundary, an echo, a leader. --ill-dash-connector = 8 6 for a blue connector between objects, which ends in a small solid blue diamond. A dashed edge is context, a limit, or something not yet built. A solid edge is a thing that exists.
TextureA dot grid marks a raw or unresolved surface: dots of 2.2 units at 30% line colour, on a 28–40 unit pitch. Hatching at 13% marks a cut or standing face. Shipped surfaces stay plain. On the solid blue plane the dots are paper at 40%.
CornersSquare. The only curves are circles and ellipses that are themselves the form: an intent ring, a cylinder.
GroundTransparent, so the page's ground shows through. An illustration never sits inside a box: no hairline, no panel, no frame and no shadow, in any placement — its edges are its own lines. The source is a 1000 × 1000 canvas with the object filling 70–80% of it; that is the working file, not a placement. Each placement's ratio and subject area are set where the placement is (§9, §9a), and a placement that crops mechanically (BlockCard, ListCard) still crops, so an illustration for a card is recomposed at the card's ratio. Colours are chosen for the ground it is placed on (below); an illustration for a second ground is a second export from the same source.
FiguresNone. A person is represented by their place — a seat, a station, a marked tile — never by a body. People are photographed (§3).

4d · Illustration · Method

From brief to illustration

The brief (§10) is answered in this order. Each step fixes one part of the illustration.

№StepWhat it fixes
01The ideaThe concept in two or three words, which become the concept chip, and the message line (§4). Both are filed with the asset.
02The itemThe one thing that carries the idea through the system. It becomes the peridot element.
03The systemThe forms that hold the item (§4b), and which surface is the solid blue plane.
04The routeWhere the item is solid and where it is an echo. Paths or a thread connect them where they sit on different layers.
05The detailThe remaining surfaces, filled sparingly with the UI shorthand. Shipped surfaces stay plain.
06The labelsThe concept chip pointing at the solid item, and up to two part labels. Then the checks in §4, labels off.

Checklist

□One idea, and one carried item that expresses it.
□Every peridot mark is the carried item, solid once and echoed elsewhere, or the concept chip's block that points at it.
□Exactly one solid blue plane, built from the forms and the UI shorthand.
□Every edge projected from plane coordinates; every outline 1.5px, non-scaling. Planes flat; thickness only on a plinth, the carried item or a cylinder.
□Dashed for proposed, solid for built.
□No gradients, shadows, perspective or new hues.
□One concept chip, up to two part labels, on a removable layer.

Caption and altNo caption on either kind (§7). A product-UI cover takes alt text naming the product and the part; an illustration cover takes empty alt, because the card title or the h1 already names what it stands for.
Ground--color-paper or --color-paper-deep. A cover or banner is not placed on ink or blue, so an illustration needs one export per light ground — two, paper and paper-deep, because the occluding fill is the ground's own colour (§4a) — and a cut-out is checked against light grounds only. A cut-out whose edge does not hold takes the contrast panel in --color-paper or --color-paper-deep (§2a); a part whose edge holds against neither is not a cover.

Choosing the kind and the subject

Run the steps in order and stop at the first one that returns an answer. Every input is a field on the case-study record, so the choice can be made, and checked, without taste.

StepRule
InputsFrom the case-study record: engagement activity, product domain, industry, primary use, UI available (shipped or client-approved, and cleared for use per §10), and what changed — one noun phrase naming what the project changed ("the one record every team reads", "the handoff from design file to component").
0 · Product UI?The cover is product UI when all three hold: UI is available; what changed is a part of the product rather than a process around it; and a cut-out of that part passes both checks below (nameable at 326px, one recognisable shape at 160px). Otherwise the cover is an illustration, and steps 1–4 choose its archetype.
1 · ActivityIf the engagement's primary deliverable is not a product surface — research, an audit, a design-to-code setup, a design-system setup, a modernisation plan — the archetype is the activity. The concept is the work that was done, not the product it was done to.
2 · DomainOtherwise, if the product belongs to a domain in the registry below, the archetype is the domain.
3 · IndustryOtherwise — the product is horizontal, or its domain is not registered — if the industry shaped the design, the archetype is the industry. "Shaped" is checkable: the case study's challenge names an industry-specific constraint (regulation, site conditions, clinical safety, market hours).
4 · UseOtherwise the archetype is how people use it: at a desk with dense data, in the field on a phone, together in real time, or in conversation with a model.
ModifierWhere steps 1–3 answered, the primary use may add one modifier: one secondary object or arrangement from its row. It qualifies the subject and is never a second subject. At most one.
EmphasisIllustration: the carried item, solid once (§4b), is what changed. Product UI: the part in frame is what changed, and nothing of ours marks it — no ring, no highlight; the product's own accent does the work (§2a).
No row fitsStop. Add a registry row — motif, default emphasis and a sample message line — and have it approved before anything is made. An agent never invents a motif outside the registry.

Composition and responsive behaviour

RulePass condition
BudgetIllustration: one motif, at most one modifier, one emphasis; one solid blue plane and one carried item (§4b). Product UI: one part on the cover, so the same asset serves the ListCard thumbnail. On the banner, a cut-out of up to five parts (§2a). Where a check fails, objects or parts come out — lines are never thinned and nothing is shrunk to fit (§4, §2a).
Cover subject areaEverything that carries the concept sits inside the central 4:3 of the 3:2 frame — the tightest crop any card applies (BlockCard horizontal). The outer strips carry ground or bleed only, so any crop lands on them.
Banner subject areaNo placement crops the banner. The subject keeps clear space of at least a tenth of the frame's height on every side, and spans 45–70% of the frame's height, so the frame never reads as empty at the full column. A cut-out may bleed off one or two edges per §2a.
AxisA subject with no reading direction centres on the frame's vertical axis. One with a direction — a flow, before and after — runs left to right.
Checked at 160pxThe cover at ListCard thumbnail width: the subject is one recognisable shape. Illustration: the emphasis is a distinct peridot mark at least 6px on its shortest side. Product UI: the part's silhouette reads as one object.
Checked at 326pxThe cover in a one-track card, and the banner in PageHero, at the 390px viewport's column. Illustration: a reader shown the registry names the right row within a few seconds; no two objects merge; no gap between two lines is under 3px. Product UI: the part is nameable (the pick test, §2) and clears the §2a scale floor.
One file serves every widthIllustration: SVG, 1.5px at every size by vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke (§4a). Product UI: a cut-out mastered as PNG with alpha and served as WebP with alpha (§8). No mobile-only crop (§2).
Across a pageWhere a page lists more than one cover (a listing, related work, home), no two adjacent covers share an archetype, and no two adjacent product-UI covers show the same kind of part. Where the set makes that impossible, reorder the listing; never change a cover's subject to force the variety.

10 · Sourcing, rights, governance

Where images come from, and who says yes

RulePass condition
SourcingClient work is captured from the product. Team and studio are shot by the team. Illustration is generated from a brief (below). Stock photography is not published; every category is first-party. A credited stand-in may appear inside an internal document, never on a public surface, and is replaced by a first-party photograph before publication.
RecordsNo licence record is kept for imagery; nothing is licensed in. Two records are kept because other rules depend on them: client approval where the row below requires it, and team consent (§3). Both are filed with the asset.
Client approvalRequired, in writing, before any public use of client work: shipped or not, credited or not, and for every caption that names the client.
Client logos · sourceUsed only with the written approval above, and only as the file the client supplies or publishes, as SVG. Never redrawn, retyped, traced or recoloured beyond the rule below.
Client logos · colourIn a set of marks — a logo strip, a client wall — every mark is one colour: the ground's ink, --color-neutral-900 on paper and paper-deep, --color-white on ink. Never the clients' own colours inside a set, never a tint or an opacity. A mark shown alone, beside its own case study, may be the client's full-colour version.
Client logos · sizeMarks in a set are sized to equal visual weight, not equal width: each is scaled so its bounding-box area matches the set's target area, then capped at the set's maximum height and width. A mark with unusually heavy or light strokes may be corrected by up to ±15% of that size, and the correction is recorded with the asset. The gap between marks belongs to the layout (on the website, the Cluster), never to a logo.
Client logos · neverOn an image, in a box or tile of its own, with a shadow, cropped, or animated.
Team consentPer §3: written, scoped to surfaces, reviewable before publication, revocable. Leavers: withdrawn from active surfaces at the next deploy unless they opt in to remain; withdrawn on request at any time.
AI-generated imageryPermitted for illustration only. Each generation starts from a written brief that includes: the §4a direction verbatim, the message line (§4), the subject as a noun phrase, the placement and its smallest size, the palette tokens, and the exclusions in §4. Output is checked against §4 and §4a row by row before use. Not permitted for client work (the product is the source) or team and studio (the team is the source). Retouching that adds no content (dust, sensor spots) is permitted in any category; confidential values are replaced before capture (§2). No disclosure is made, in captions or anywhere else (decided 25 September 2026).
VerificationNo internal review gate beyond written client approval (§10), team consent (§3) and the cover registry (§9a). Whoever places an image checks it against §2, §3, §4 and §6 before use, row by row. The person who places it is responsible for it.
Changes to this chapterNo fixed review. It changes when a rule fails in use, or when a placement it does not yet cover is built.
